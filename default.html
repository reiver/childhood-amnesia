Childhood Amnesia

why you cannot remember being a baby — and how far back you can remember, depending on how old you are now

by Charles Iliya Krempeaux

Childhood Amnesia is the name for the fact that adults remember almost nothing from the first few years of their lives.

Ask an adult for their earliest memory, and, on average, it will be from when they were about 3 to 3½ years old. Before that, there is (for most people) almost nothing. And, from 3½ to about 7, there is only a little — much less than you would expect.

(Childhood amnesia is also called infantile amnesia. Some researchers use infantile amnesia for the period from birth to about age 2, and childhood amnesia for the period from about 2 to 7. This article uses childhood amnesia for the whole thing.)

But, here is the surprising part:

Children do not have the same childhood amnesia that adults do. A 5-year-old can often remember things that happened when they were 1 or 2. Those memories are forgotten later — mostly between the ages of about 7 and 11.

So, childhood amnesia is not just about which memories get made. It is also about which memories get kept. And, the answer to “how far back can a person remember?” depends on how old that person is right now.

This article explains what is known about that — age by age.

Two Different Ages

When talking about childhood amnesia, there are always two ages involved:

  1. the age a person was when something happened to them, and
  2. the age a person is now, when they are trying to remember it.

Most of what people say about childhood amnesia — “nobody remembers anything before age 3” — is only about the first age. It quietly assumes the second age is adult.

But, the answer changes a lot depending on the second age. For example:

  • a 5-year-old may well remember something that happened when they were 2, but
  • a 25-year-old almost certainly does not remember that same event.

The memory existed. It was later lost.

Two Different Questions

There are also two different questions researchers ask. And, they get different kinds of answers.

“What is your earliest memory?”

This finds the edge — how far back a person's memory reaches. Researchers call this edge the offset of childhood amnesia (the point where the amnesia ends). For adults, it is about 3 to 3½ years.

“How much do you remember from age X?”

This finds the density — how many memories a person has from each age. Even past the edge, adults have very few memories from ages 3, 4, and 5, and the number grows slowly each year after that.

A person can have an earliest memory from age 3, and still remember almost nothing else from age 3. So, the edge and the density are both needed to describe what someone remembers.

Adults

Let us start with adults, since that is where most research has been done, and where the numbers are most settled.

The Edge

The average adult's earliest memory is from about 3 to 3½ years old. This has been found over and over — going back more than 100 years.

  • A survey published around 1900, by the French psychologists Victor and Catherine Henri, found an average earliest memory of 3 years 1 month.
  • A 2018 study of 6,641 adults (Akhtar and colleagues) found an average of 3.2 years.
  • A study that asked the same 36 adults for their earliest memory once a year, for 4 years (Bauer, Tasdemir-Ozdes, and Larkina, 2014), found an average of 38.9 months (about 3 years 3 months) — and it barely changed from year to year.

Researchers often summarize all of this as “3½ years”.

But, there is a lot of variation between people. In that 4-year study, people's earliest memories ranged from 9 months to 60 months (5 years). About 12% had an earliest memory from before age 2. About 15% had an earliest memory from age 5.

The Density

In 2000, the psychologist David Rubin pooled together more than 11,000 childhood memories from adults, collected by many earlier studies. He then counted how many of those memories came from each year of childhood.

Here is how adults' memories from before age 8 are spread out over those 8 years:

Notice that:

  • memories from before age 2 are extremely rare (about 1 in 200),
  • memories from age 2 are rare,
  • from age 3 the number starts rising quickly, and
  • it keeps rising until about age 7.

Rubin put it this way:

Of the autobiographical memories reported as occurring before age 11, 1.1% occurred before age 3, with a sharp rise after that point.

Rubin also found that this pattern was nearly the same for men and women, for people in their 20s, their 30s to 50s, and their 60s to 70s. In other words, once a person is an adult, their childhood amnesia stops changing. A 70-year-old's earliest memories reach back about as far as a 20-year-old's.

(Rubin's data was all from the United States. He noted that data from other cultures “would almost certainly be different.” See Culture below.)

Adults Keep the Same Earliest Memory

Adults are also consistent. Ask an adult for their earliest memory, then ask again a year later, and most of the time you get the same memory. In the 4-year study, 72% to 82% of adults named the same event as their earliest memory each time.

As we will see, this is not true of children.

Children

For a long time, almost all childhood amnesia research asked adults about their childhoods. Starting around 2005, researchers began asking children — and following the same children over many years. What they found changed how childhood amnesia is understood.

Young Children Remember Further Back

Young children's earliest memories are much earlier than adults' are.

  • In a 2010 study by Karen Tustin and Harlene Hayne, children's earliest memories were, on average, from “between one and two years” old — compared to about 3½ for the adults. And, more than 20% of the children's memories were from before their first birthday. (The 5-year-olds' average earliest memory was about 18 months.)
  • Carole Peterson, who has studied this for decades, found that 3- and 4-year-olds “can readily recall events from their second year” (that is, from when they were 1).
  • In a 2008 study by Emily Cleveland and Elaine Reese, children aged 5½ could recall about half of the events, from before age 2, that they had talked about with their mothers back then.

So, a 5-year-old has memories that a 25-year-old does not. They have not yet forgotten them.

The Forgetting Starts Around 7

The clearest evidence for when these early memories disappear comes from a study by Patricia Bauer and Marina Larkina, published in 2014.

When the children were 3 years old, their mothers talked with them about six one-time events from their recent past. Then, years later, the researchers came back and asked the children about those same events. Different groups of children were asked at different ages:

Something happens between 7 and 8. The researchers called it “the onset of childhood amnesia in childhood”.

They also found something odd. The 8- and 9-year-olds remembered fewer of the events. But, for the events they did remember, they gave more detail — and told it more like an adult would.

(A note about “5-year-olds”: in this study, the “5-year-old” group was actually, on average, 4 years 9 months old; the “6-year-olds” were on average 5 years 10 months; and so on. Research groups are often named by rounding. When ages change fast — as they do at the start of life — a few months can matter.)

Later Memories Survive Better

The forgetting that begins around age 7 does not hit all early memories equally. It hits the earliest ones the hardest.

A 1998 study by Robyn Fivush and April Schwarzmueller found that 8-year-olds remembered:

  • 77% of events from when they were 3½ to 4, and
  • 92% of events from when they were 5 to 6.

Compare those with the roughly 35% of age-3 events that 8- and 9-year-olds remembered in Bauer and Larkina's study. Each half-year makes a difference.

Adult-Like by About 10 to 13

After the forgetting starts, the edge of a child's memory moves later — until it settles at the adult value.

  • Carole Peterson, Valerie Grant, and Lesley Boland (2005) asked 136 people aged 6 to 19 for their earliest memories. The 6- to 9-year-olds remembered things from when they were about 3. But, from about age 10 on, there was no more change: the earliest memories were from about 3½, the same as adults. As Peterson put it, “there's a striking similarity in the age of the earliest memory for adults and ten-year-olds.”
  • Bauer and Larkina also found that 7- to 11-year-olds' earliest memories were from about 3 years 8 months, on average — already about the adult value.
  • Tustin and Hayne found that 12- to 13-year-olds were close to adults.

So, roughly: childhood amnesia starts taking away early memories around age 7, and has mostly finished by around age 10 to 13.

Children's Earliest Memories Keep Changing

Unlike adults, children do not keep the same earliest memory.

Peterson, Kelly Warren, and Megan Short (2011) asked 140 children aged 4 to 13 for their three earliest memories, and then asked them again 2 years later:

Peterson described it this way:

Younger children's earliest memories seemed to change, with memories from younger ages being replaced by memories from older ages.

A young child's “earliest memory” is a moving target. It slides later and later, as older memories are lost. It stops sliding (mostly) in the early teens.

The Table

So, to answer the question “at age X, which ages can a person remember?”:

  • At 3 to 5, a child can reach back to age 1 or 2 — sometimes even before their first birthday.
  • At 6 to 7, they still have most of their memories from age 3, and some from earlier.
  • At 8 to 9, most memories from age 3 and earlier are gone; memories from age 4 and later mostly remain.
  • From about 10 to 13 onward, the edge is about 3½ — just like an adult — and stays there for life.

Months, Not Just Years

Notice that whole-number ages are not fine enough at the start of life.

The usual adult edge — “3½” — is itself a fraction. 38.9 months is about 3 years and 3 months. And, the difference between an event at 3 and an event at 3½ can be the difference between a memory that is probably gone and one that is probably kept.

Researchers who study young children measure ages in months. For example, Cleveland and Reese's study asked about events from when the children were 19, 25, 32, 40, and 65 months old.

After about age 7, though, a few months makes much less difference — and whole years (or even several years) are enough to describe the pattern. And, for adults, the pattern does not change at all between their 20s and their 70s.

Why Children Forget Faster

Everyone forgets. But, children forget faster than adults do.

In a 4-year study (Bauer and Larkina, 2016), researchers compared 4-, 6-, and 8-year-olds with adults. Each person was asked about events from their own life, 1, 2, or 3 years after the events happened. After just 1 year, without any prompting:

  • adults failed to recall only 3% of the events, while
  • 4-year-olds failed to recall 61%.

The 6- and 8-year-olds were in between. The older the child, the slower the forgetting.

Two Shapes of Forgetting

There is also a difference in the shape of the forgetting.

For adults, forgetting slows down over time. A memory that has survived 10 years is likely to survive 10 more. (Mathematicians call this kind of curve a power function.)

For children (through about the first 10 years of life), forgetting does not slow down. Each year, a child loses about the same fraction of whatever memories are left — like a radioactive substance with a fixed half-life. (Mathematicians call this kind of curve an exponential function.)

So, a child's early memories are under steady pressure, year after year. Bauer and Larkina wrote:

Accelerated forgetting throughout childhood results in an ever-shrinking pool of memories from early in life. The resulting distribution of memories reveals childhood amnesia in the making.

As children get older, their forgetting slows down and becomes more adult-like. By the time it does, most of the earliest memories are already gone.

Better Stories Last Longer

Memories that are told as a coherent story — with a beginning, middle, and end, and a reason why it mattered — last longer.

In the 2016 study, events that were first described as a coherent story were almost twice as likely to be remembered later. But, only about 28% of 4-year-olds' descriptions were coherent, compared with 57% of 8-year-olds' and 84% of adults'.

Young children's memories tend to be sparse and loosely organized. That may be part of why they are lost.

What Is Going On?

There is not one single accepted explanation for childhood amnesia. It probably has several causes, working together. Here are the main ideas researchers have proposed.

A Growing Brain

The hippocampus is a part of the brain that is important for forming new memories of events. In early childhood, it is still developing, and many new neurons are being added to it.

In experiments with mice, adding new neurons to the hippocampus made the mice forget things they had learned earlier (Akers and colleagues, 2014). The idea is that new neurons, as they wire themselves in, disrupt the connections that hold older memories. Young brains add new neurons very quickly — so, young brains may forget quickly.

Other mouse experiments (Guskjolen and colleagues, 2018) were able to bring back a “forgotten” infant memory by directly stimulating the neurons that had stored it. This suggests that at least some early memories may not be erased, but instead become impossible to find.

Language

Young children have few words. And, memories that were stored before a child had the words for them seem to stay without words.

In studies by Gabrielle Simcock and Harlene Hayne (2002), young children who experienced an unusual event (a “magic shrinking machine”) could later describe it only with words they had already known at the time it happened — even if they had learned more words since.

A Sense of Self

An autobiographical memory is a memory of something that happened to me. That requires a sense of “me” — and a sense that “me” continues through time. This understanding develops gradually in the preschool years.

Talking About the Past

Children learn how to remember partly by talking about the past with adults.

Children whose parents talk with them about past events in a rich, detailed way — asking questions, adding details, and asking what the child thought and felt — tend, later, to have earlier earliest memories. One study (Jack, MacDonald, Reese, and Hayne, 2009) followed children from early childhood into their teens, and found that the way their mothers had talked with them, when they were about 2 to 3½, predicted how early their earliest memories were when they were teenagers.

Both Sides

Patricia Bauer (2015) has argued that childhood amnesia comes from changes on both sides:

  • young children store memories less well (fewer details, less organized, less connected to other memories), and
  • young children forget faster.

As children grow, they get better at storing memories, and slower at forgetting them. The earliest memories got the worst of both.

Complications

Dating Errors

All of this research depends on knowing when a remembered event happened. And, people are not very good at that.

Qi Wang and Carole Peterson (2014, 2016) followed children for 8 years. They found that as children got older, they dated the very same memories as having happened later than they had said before. In one group, the average age of children's earliest memory went from 35.8 months, to 40.0 months 2 years later, to 52.5 months 8 years later. Some memories were dated more than a year later than originally.

Part of the reason the edge seems to move later during childhood, then, is not that memories are lost — but that they are mis-dated. Wang and Peterson suggest that the usual estimate of adults' earliest memories may be wrong, and that the real adult edge might be closer to 2½ than 3½.

False Earliest Memories

In the 2018 study of 6,641 adults, about 39% said their earliest memory was from age 2 or younger. And, 893 people said their earliest memory was from age 1 or younger.

The researchers (Shazia Akhtar, Martin Conway, and colleagues) think most of these are probably fictional — built, without the person realizing it, from photographs, family stories, and knowledge of what babies do. Such memories feel real to the people who have them.

So, an adult who claims to remember being 1 might be right. But, it is more likely that the memory was constructed later.

How You Ask Matters

The answer people give depends on the question.

In one set of studies by Fiona Jack and Harlene Hayne, young adults were asked for memories in different ways:

  • asked for any memory, their earliest was from about 6 years 7 months,
  • asked for childhood memories, their earliest was from about 5 years 6 months,
  • asked specifically for their earliest memory, it was from about 3 years 9 months.

And, it matters what counts as a “memory”. Jack and Hayne (2010) found that if you include fragments — a single image, a feeling, a moment with no story around it — adults' earliest memories are earlier (around age 2) than if you count only full memories of events. They describe childhood amnesia as having two stages:

  • a dense amnesia, up to about age 2, with no memories (or only isolated fragments), and
  • a lighter amnesia, up to about age 5 to 7, with fragments and some memories, but much less than later in life.

This is also why studies of children disagree with each other somewhat. Tustin and Hayne showed children photographs of themselves at different ages, and worked backwards from the present — a method that helps people reach further back. Their children reported earlier memories than children in other studies that just asked.

Culture

Almost all of this research was done in Western countries — mostly the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and New Zealand.

And, culture matters. For example:

  • in New Zealand, Māori adults (whose culture places a strong emphasis on the past and on family stories) report earlier earliest memories than Pākehā (European New Zealand) or Chinese adults,
  • in one study, Chinese women's earliest memories averaged about 6 years,
  • women, on average, report slightly earlier memories than men.

So, the specific ages in this article are best thought of as describing Western children and adults. The overall pattern — children remember further back than adults, and lose those memories during middle childhood — is what is most likely to hold everywhere.

Summary

  • Adults' earliest memories are, on average, from about 3 to 3½ years old. And, they have very few memories from ages 3 to 7.
  • But, young children remember further back — often to age 1 or 2.
  • Those early memories are lost during middle childhood. The forgetting starts around age 7.
  • By about age 10 to 13, the edge of memory has reached the adult value of about 3½. It then stays there for the rest of life.
  • Children forget faster than adults, and their forgetting does not slow down over time the way adults' does.
  • The exact numbers depend on how people are asked, how “memory” is defined, dating errors, and culture.

In short: childhood amnesia is not something you are born with. It is something that happens to you — in childhood.
