Remembering a Parent Who Left

what a child keeps — and loses — of a parent who left when they were young

by Charles Iliya Krempeaux

This article is an example of how childhood amnesia is likely to play out for a real-world scenario.

It asks a simple question:

If a parent leaves when their child is 4 years old — or 3 years old — how much will that child remember of that parent at age 8? 9? 10? 11? 12? 13? 14? And as an adult?

The short answer:

If the parent left when the child was 4: at 8, the child probably still has several memories of the parent — mostly from the last year before the parent left. Between 8 and about 11, most of those memories fade. From about 12 to 14 onward, the child will probably have only a handful of memories of the parent — and those few will tend to stay for life.

If the parent left when the child was 3: the same thing happens, but it ends with less. At 8, the child probably still has a few memories of the parent. By about 10 or 11, most or all of them are gone. As an adult, the child will most likely have no specific memories of the parent — or perhaps one or two fragments.

Just one year makes that much difference. The rest of this article explains why.

(If you only care about one of the two ages, you can jump to If the Parent Left at 4 or If the Parent Left at 3. And, to see them compared, see Side by Side.)

Before We Start

There are three things to keep in mind.

First: no study has followed children whose parent left at age 3 or 4, and measured what they remember of that parent at each later age. What this article does is take what is known about childhood memory in general, and apply it to this situation. So, it describes a likely pattern, not a prediction for any one child.

Second: the research is about memories of specific events — “the time we went to the beach”, “the day she gave me the red boots”. It is not about general knowledge of a person (“he had a beard”, “she sang to me at bedtime”), recognizing their face, or the emotional effects of the relationship. So, when this article says “remember the parent”, it means remembering specific moments with them. The research says nothing, either way, about those other things.

Third: the research applies to any parent, or any caregiver — mother, father, grandparent, or someone else. Nothing in it is specific to mothers or to fathers.

The Key Idea: The Edge of Memory

Adults remember almost nothing from before about age 3 to 3½. Call that the edge of memory.

But, young children do not have that edge yet. A 5-year-old can remember things from when they were 1 or 2. Those early memories are lost later — mostly between about age 7 and age 11. By about age 10 to 13, a child's edge of memory has moved to where an adult's is — about 3½ — and it stays there for life.

So, what matters most is where the time with the parent falls, compared to that edge:

If the Parent Left at 4

What the Child Has at Age 4

By age 4, the child has spent about 4 years with the parent. That is a lot of experience.

But, most of it is already out of reach — even at age 4.

Young children forget fast. In one study (Bauer and Larkina, 2016), 4-year-olds could not recall, on their own, 61% of events from just 1 year earlier. (Adults could not recall only 3%.)

So, the memories of the parent that a 4-year-old can actually recall come mostly from the most recent year or so — roughly from age 3 to age 4. There may also be some memories from age 1 or 2. (Children aged 3 to 4 can recall events from when they were 1.)

Age 5 to 7

Not much changes yet.

Children from 5 to 7 still remember a lot from their early years. In one study (Bauer and Larkina, 2014), children at ages 5, 6, and 7 still remembered 60% or more of events from when they were 3.

So, at ages 5 to 7, the child probably still has many of their memories of the parent. (Though, some will already have faded — young children forget fast.)

Age 8

Age 7 to 8 is the turning point. This is when early memories start to disappear quickly.

In the studies:

  • 8-year-olds remembered only about 36% of events from age 3,
  • but 8-year-olds remembered about 77% of events from age 3½ to 4.

So, at 8, the child probably still has a fair number of memories of the parent — mostly from the last half-year, or year, before the parent left. Memories from age 3 and earlier are mostly gone, or going.

Age 9

The thinning continues. 9-year-olds remembered about 34% of events from age 3.

The memories from age 3½ to 4 — the last months with the parent — are still the most likely to remain.

Age 10 to 11

By about age 10, children's memory has reached the adult pattern. Their earliest memories are from about age 3½ — the same as adults.

That matters here, because the last months with the parent (age 3½ to 4) sit right at that edge. And, even adults have only a few memories from that time.

So, by 10 to 11, the child's memories of the parent are probably down to a handful of specific moments, or fragments. They will most likely be from the months just before the parent left.

Age 12 to 13

About the same as at 10 to 11 — but more settled.

Young children's earliest memories keep changing. Ask a 4- or 5-year-old for their earliest memory, and ask again 2 years later, and only 7% give the same memory. But, by 12 to 13, 39% give the same memory.

So, by 12 to 13, the few memories of the parent that remain are becoming stable. What is left now is roughly what will be left for life.

Age 14 and Up

Adult-like, and stable.

Expect a few memories, or fragments, of the parent — from around age 3½ to 4. Possibly including a memory of the parent leaving (see The Leaving Itself).

These will probably stay about the same for the rest of the child's life. Adults name the same earliest memory 72% to 82% of the time, year after year. And, people in their 70s remember about as far back as people in their 20s.

Left at 4: All Together

If the Parent Left at 3

The story for a child whose parent left at 3 has the same shape as the story for age 4. The memories are kept at first, start to fade around 7 to 8, and settle by the early teens.

The difference is how it ends. All of the child's time with the parent is before the adult edge of memory. So, by the time the child's memory becomes adult-like, there is very little — possibly nothing — left.

What the Child Has at Age 3

By age 3, the child has spent about 3 years with the parent.

As with age 4, most of that is already out of reach. The memories of the parent that a 3-year-old can recall come mostly from the most recent year or so — roughly from age 2 to age 3. There may also be some memories from age 1.

And, a 3-year-old has less language than a 4-year-old. Memories formed before a child has the words for them tend to be harder to put into words later.

Age 4 to 7

At first, many of the memories are still there.

Children aged 5 to 7 still remembered 60% or more of events from when they were 3, in the study by Bauer and Larkina (2014). And, children aged 5½ remembered about half of events from before age 2, in a study by Cleveland and Reese (2008).

So, during these years, the child probably still has a number of memories of the parent — especially from the months just before the parent left. (Though, young children forget fast, and some will already be gone.)

Age 8 to 9

The turning point.

8-year-olds remembered only about 36% of events from age 3. 9-year-olds, about 34%. Memories from age 2 and earlier fare even worse.

So, at 8 to 9, the child probably has only a few memories of the parent left — mostly from around age 3.

Age 10 to 11

By about age 10, the child's edge of memory has reached about 3½ — the adult value.

For this child, all of the time with the parent is before that edge. So, by 10 to 11, the child probably has no specific memories of the parent left — or perhaps one or two fragments.

Age 12 and Up

Whatever is left — if anything — becomes stable, and will tend to stay for life.

Most likely, that is no specific memories of the parent at all. Perhaps a single fragment — an image, a moment, a feeling, with no story around it. Possibly a memory of the parent leaving (see The Leaving Itself).

But, people differ. About 12% of adults have an earliest memory from before age 2. So, some children whose parent left at 3 will keep a real memory or two of that parent.

How Sparse Are Memories from Ages 2, 3, and 4?

Memories from ages 2, 3, and 4 exist for adults. But, there are not many of them.

So, an adult has about 2½ times as many memories from age 4 as from age 3 — and about 8 times as many from age 4 as from age 2.

That is the difference between the two cases in this article. A child whose parent left at 4 has, as an adult, memories of that parent from a sparse part of childhood. A child whose parent left at 3 has, as an adult, memories of that parent only from an almost empty part of childhood.

The Leaving Itself

The day the parent left — or the time around it — is the one event most likely to be remembered.

Emotional, life-changing, one-time events are remembered from earlier ages than everyday ones. JoNell Usher and Ulric Neisser (1993) found that adults could remember:

  • the birth of a younger sibling, or a stay in the hospital, from as early as about age 2, and
  • a death in the family, or moving to a new home, from as early as about age 3.

A child of 4 is past those ages. So, if the parent left at 4, some memory of the leaving has a reasonable chance of lasting.

A child of 3 is right at those ages. So, if the parent left at 3, a memory of the leaving is possible — but less likely to last than at 4.

Stress can also play a part. A study of young adults whose parents had separated (Artioli and Reese, 2014) found that those who felt more stress and pain about the separation had earlier — but sparser — childhood memories.

What Changes the Numbers

The patterns above are for a typical child. Several things can change them. These apply to both ages.

Talking About the Past

This is probably the biggest one.

Memories that get talked about last longer. And, memories that are told as a clear story — with a beginning, a middle, and an end — last much longer. In one study, memories first told as a clear story were almost twice as likely to be remembered later.

In several of the studies used in this article, the early events were talked about at the time — with a parent, or with a researcher. Normally, a parent is one of the main people who talks with a young child about the past. A child whose parent has left loses one of those people. So, the child may keep fewer memories than the study numbers suggest.

Unless someone else steps in. If another parent, a grandparent, or another relative talks with the child about the parent who left, more memories may survive. Artioli and Reese (2014) found that when parents separated before the child was 7, earlier memories went with having ties to extended family, and with being able to tell early memories as clear stories.

Contact After Leaving

If the parent stays in contact — visits, calls, holidays — then the child keeps making new memories of them. And, seeing the parent can bring back older memories.

In that case, the later memories will mix with — and often replace — the early ones. The patterns in this article are for a parent who leaves and is not seen again for a long time.

Photographs and Family Stories

Photographs and family stories can help keep some memories alive.

But, they can also create memories that were never there. A child can look at a photograph many times, hear the story behind it, and later feel that they remember that moment — when what they actually remember is the photograph.

In a 2018 study of 6,641 adults (Akhtar and colleagues), about 39% said their earliest memory was from age 2 or younger. The researchers think most of those memories are probably constructed in this way. They feel completely real to the people who have them.

This matters more if the parent left at 3. Since few real memories of the parent are likely to survive, any “memories” of the parent that an adult has are more likely to have been built from photographs and stories.

Getting the Age Wrong

As children grow up, they tend to think their early memories happened later than they really did (Wang and Peterson, 2014, 2016). Some memories get dated more than a year later than they first were.

So, a teenager might say “I think I was 5” about a memory of the parent — when the parent actually left when they were 3 or 4. The memory may be real, and just mis-dated.

Summary

  • A child whose parent leaves at 3 or 4 has many memories of the parent at that time — but mostly from the last year or so.
  • Those memories mostly survive until about age 7.
  • From about 7 to 11, most of them fade.
  • If the parent left at 4: from about 12 to 14 onward, a handful of memories usually remain — and those tend to stay for life.
  • If the parent left at 3: as an adult, the child will most likely have no specific memories of the parent — or perhaps a fragment or two.
  • The difference comes from the adult edge of memory, at about 3 to 3½: time with the parent after the edge can be remembered; time before it is almost always lost.
  • The day the parent left is one of the memories most likely to last — more likely at 4 than at 3.
  • Talking with the child about the parent helps memories last. Photographs and stories help too — but can also create memories that were never there.
  • All of this is about memories of specific events. It does not measure what the child knows about the parent, or how the relationship affected them.
